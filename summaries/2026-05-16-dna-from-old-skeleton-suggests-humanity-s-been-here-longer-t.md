# DNA from old skeleton suggests humanity’s been here longer than we thought

## Abstract
A 2,000-year-old Stone Age genome from southern Africa indicates that the Khoe-San lineage split from other modern human groups around 300,000 years ago, pushing the likely origin of humanity far earlier than previous genetic estimates.

## Introduction
Determining when anatomically modern humans first appeared is complicated by partial fossils and the coexistence of archaic relatives like Neanderthals. Earlier genetic work pointed to a divergence of the Khoe-San hunter-gatherers roughly 100,000–150,000 years ago, but recent fossil finds and doubts about later population mixing cast uncertainty on that timeline. A new study obtains DNA from a Stone Age skeleton that predates major migrations in the region, offering a cleaner window into early human divergence and potentially rewriting the deep history of our species.

## Main Points
- The Khoe-San people of southern Africa carry the highest genetic diversity of any living human population, previously used to date their split from other modern humans at 100,000–150,000 years ago.
- Recent fossil claims and the massive Bantu expansion into southern Africa raised questions about whether the Khoe-San had remained genetically isolated; any later admixture would skew the old estimates.
- Researchers sequenced DNA from three Stone Age southern African skeletons that predate the Bantu arrival. Only one yielded a high-quality genome, but it clustered unambiguously with the Khoe-San.
- Modern Khoe-San genomes were shown to carry additional ancestry from an East African population that had already intermixed with Eurasians, meaning they have experienced gene flow since their initial divergence.
- Comparing the Stone Age genome to other modern populations yielded much older split dates: 285,000–365,000 years, with the deepest split between the Khoe-San lineage and West Africa’s Mandinka people at around 356,000 years.
