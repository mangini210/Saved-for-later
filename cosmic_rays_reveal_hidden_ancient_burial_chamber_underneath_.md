# Cosmic rays reveal hidden ancient burial chamber underneath Naples

**URL:** https://arstechnica.com/?p=1939194
**Category:** history
**Date:** 2026-05-16
**Type:** Long Summary

---

## Abstract
Using muon tomography, a non-invasive imaging technique that leverages naturally occurring cosmic-ray muons, scientists discovered a previously hidden ancient burial chamber buried 10 meters beneath modern-day Naples.

## Introduction
The ancient Hellenistic necropolis of Neapolis lies under modern Naples, Italy, but its location in a densely populated urban district makes traditional excavation methods like bore holing or seismic surveys impractical. Muography (muon tomography) offers a non-invasive alternative by harnessing the steady supply of high-energy muons produced by cosmic rays, similar to X-ray imaging but capable of penetrating dense rock and stone. This technique, which has a storied history of searching for hidden chambers in Egyptian and Mayan pyramids and analyzing the structure of Florence's cathedral dome, was applied to the Sanità district necropolis to reveal a tomb unseen for centuries.

## Main Points
- **Muon Imaging Technique:** Nuclear emulsion detectors—simple, compact devices requiring no external power—recorded muon tracks as they passed through and were absorbed by underground structures. Denser materials block more muons, creating a shadow effect; hidden chambers appear because they block fewer particles.
- **Site History:** The Neapolis necropolis in the Sanità district was carved from soft volcanic tuff rock and used for burials from the late 4th century BCE to early 1st century CE. It was subsequently buried under sediment from natural disasters, notably "lava dei vergini" mud-and-rock floods.
- **Previous Discoveries:** Four tombs (Ipogeo dei Cristallini) were found in the late 19th century, and two more (Ipogeo dei Togati and Ipogeo dei Melograni) were discovered after a 1980 earthquake, raising hopes of locating additional chambers.
- **Experiment Setup:** Two emulsion detector modules were placed in an underground cellar (formerly used to age ham) from March 10 to April 7, 2018, and the developed films were compared against a 3D laser-scanning model of known accessible underground structures.
- **New Discovery:** An excess of muons revealed a rectangular structure measuring 6.5 by 11.5 feet (2 by 3.5 meters), consistent with a burial chamber likely dating to the late 4th/early 3rd century BCE, probably the tomb of a wealthy individual or family.

## Conclusions
Muography successfully imaged a hidden burial chamber in an archaeologically sensitive urban area where conventional excavation is unfeasible, confirming its value as a non-invasive prospection tool. The rectangular chamber, partially filled with rock and sediment, expands knowledge of the Hellenistic necropolis and suggests more such tombs may await discovery beneath the modern city.

## Interesting Facts & Data
- The burial chamber sits roughly 33 feet (10 meters) below today's street level in a dense Naples neighborhood.
- The "lava dei vergini" that buried the necropolis was not volcanic lava but a slurry of mud and rocks loosened from hillsides during heavy rains.
- Nuclear emulsion technology was first developed from Ernest Rutherford's early 20th-century radiation studies and later led to the discovery of the pi-meson.
- The detectors were placed in a converted cellar originally used for aging ham.
- Muon imaging was famously pioneered by physicist Luis Alvarez in the 1960s to search for chambers in Egypt's Pyramid of Khafre.
