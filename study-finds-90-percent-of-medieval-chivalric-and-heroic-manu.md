# Study finds 90 percent of medieval chivalric and heroic manuscripts have been lost

**URL:** https://arstechnica.com/?p=1834060  
**Date:** 15 May 2026  
**Summary type:** Long Summary

---

## Abstract
Researchers successfully applied ecological "unseen species" statistical models to medieval European literature to estimate that approximately 90 percent of manuscripts and 62 percent of original chivalric and heroic works have been lost to history.

## Introduction
Scholars of ancient cultures frequently struggle with "survivorship bias," as the available historical record is limited to the small fraction of artifacts that managed to survive through the centuries. To address this, an international team of researchers adapted the "Chao1" ecological model—typically used to estimate biodiversity—to quantify the "unseen" population of lost medieval narratives. This interdisciplinary approach provides a statistical "lower bound" for the diversity of stories that once existed in Dutch, English, French, German, Icelandic, and Irish traditions.

## Main Points
*   **Methodological Innovation:** By treating literary works as "species" and individual manuscript copies as "sightings," researchers used the Chao1 formula to calculate "F0"—the number of works that likely existed but were observed zero times in the surviving record.
*   **Massive Cultural Loss:** The study estimated that of the roughly 40,614 original manuscripts across the six studied vernaculars, only 9 percent (3,649) survive today; however, the survival rate of the actual stories (works) is higher at 38 percent.
*   **Island Preservation:** Icelandic and Irish literatures show significantly higher survival rates (77% and 81% respectively), suggesting that "island ecosystems" and strong traditions of hand-copying manuscripts helped preserve culture more effectively than on the continent.
*   **The Impact of Prestige:** English literature suffered a particularly high loss rate (only 38% of works survived), likely because the Norman Conquest made French the prestigious language, leading to English manuscripts being treated as lower-value items prone to recycling.
*   **"Evenness" as a Survival Factor:** Resilience was found in "even" traditions—where many different works exist in roughly equal numbers of copies—whereas "uneven" traditions, dominated by a few popular titles, were more vulnerable to losing their rarer stories.

## Conclusions
The primary takeaway is that the survival of cultural heritage is not random but is driven by factors such as geographic isolation, social prestige, and the "evenness" of document distribution. The study proves that ecological models are versatile enough to solve complex problems in the humanities, offering a clearer picture of the "missing pieces" in the jigsaw puzzle of human history. Ultimately, understanding the scale of these losses helps researchers account for bias when interpreting the diversity of medieval society.
