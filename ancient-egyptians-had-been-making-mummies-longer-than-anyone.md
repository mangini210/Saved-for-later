# Ancient Egyptians had been making mummies longer than anyone thought

**URL:** https://arstechnica.com/science/2018/08/ancient-egyptians-had-been-making-mummies-longer-than-anyone-thought/  
**Date:** 15 May 2026  
**Summary type:** Long Summary

---

## Abstract
Chemical analysis of an intact Predynastic mummy from Upper Egypt reveals that antibacterial embalming compounds were applied around 3600 BCE, pushing back the origins of Egyptian mummification by roughly 1,500 years and showing that the practice predated the unification of Egypt.

## Introduction
The ancient Egyptians are famed for their elaborate mummification rites, but the timeline of when embalming began has been uncertain. A new interdisciplinary study of a young man’s remains, held at the Egyptian Museum in Turin, demonstrates that sophisticated preservation with plant oils, resins, and gums was already in use during the Predynastic Period. This discovery matters because it shifts the emergence of afterlife beliefs centered on bodily preservation back into the fourth millennium BCE and reveals early trade networks that brought non-native ingredients into Egypt.

## Main Points
- The Turin mummy (Predynastic, Upper Egypt) was radiocarbon-dated to ca. 3700–3500 BCE, and detailed textile analysis narrowed the range to 3650–3500 BCE, roughly 500 years before Egypt was unified under Narmer around 3100 BCE.
- Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) of organic residues on the wrappings identified a mixture of plant oil, an aromatic plant extract, a gum or sugar, and heated conifer resin—components virtually identical to those used in much later pharaonic embalming (e.g., New Kingdom, 2,500 years later).
- The conifer resin was imported, probably from the Levant (modern-day Israel), indicating Predynastic trade networks across the eastern Mediterranean.
- The body retained all its organs and showed no evidence of natron salt treatment; natural desiccation by hot, dry desert sand likely preserved it, while the antibacterial agents prevented microbial decay after burial in a simple pit.
- This is the first intact Predynastic mummy confirmed to have been deliberately treated with embalming agents; earlier work on linen fragments from Mostagedda (some dated as early as 4300 BCE) had suggested similar recipes, but those wrappings were not associated with a preserved body.
- The presence of the same embalming recipe at burials hundreds of miles apart suggests shared cultural and religious practices across Upper Egypt before state formation.
- The findings imply that the concept of an afterlife requiring physical preservation of the body was developing around 1,500 years earlier than previously thought, transforming the understanding of Predynastic belief systems.

## Conclusions
The Turin mummy provides the earliest direct evidence of intentional mummification using a recipe that later became canonical in pharaonic Egypt. This continuity indicates that core embalming knowledge and the associated religious ideology were already taking shape deep in the Predynastic Period, far earlier than written or monumental evidence suggests. It also demonstrates that early Egyptians engaged in long-distance trade to acquire embalming supplies, underlining the sophistication of their pre-state society.

## Interesting Facts & Data
- The Turin mummy belonged to a young man aged 20–30 years who had survived a healed skull fracture; he appears otherwise to have been in reasonable health.
- His burial included linen wrappings and rush matting along with grave goods like personal items and food bowls, consistent with Predynastic pit burials.
- The mummy has never been treated with modern preservatives or pesticides, which allowed uncontaminated chemical analysis.
- Conifers are not native to Egypt; the resin had to come from at least as far as the Levant, revealing an import network long before the kingdom’s unification.
- Jones noted that earlier burials from Mostagedda yielded wrappings with similar embalming residues dating back to 4300 BCE, but the bodies were discarded during 1920s excavations, making the Turin mummy the earliest intact example.
- The embalming mixture’s similarity to later recipes suggests that “the concept of an afterlife, with the physical preservation of the body at its heart, was already developing some 1,500 years earlier than previously thought” (Buckley).
